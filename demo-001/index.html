Read this bit first — it's the whole trick

You are not presenting software. You are showing them what happens to a photograph after you take it — because that's where the paperwork goes wrong for everybody in the room.

So don't tour the app. Pick one report (SW-2026-10-001) and stay on it. Twelve screens clicked quickly is forgettable. One photograph followed all the way to a client's inbox is not.

And the thing that makes it engaging is the bits: one interesting fact per beat. They're marked the bit below. Drop one in, say nothing for a second, move on.

Before they walk in — two minutes

  1. Sign in as Demonstration account. Check the header says it — if it says anything else you're in the wrong place and none of the demo data is there.
  2. Three tabs, in order: Reports, then SW-2026-10-001, then Projects.
  3. On SW-2026-10-001, get to the second tab — "2 Get PDF". That's where Publish and Share live.
    (Nearly every demo wobble is someone hunting for Share on the first tab. Thirty seconds of prep kills it.)
  4. One printed report page in your hand. Phone unlocked, on the On site screen.

The script

1

Open

0:00–0:15 · no screen

"Morning. Five minutes, and I'll spend most of it on one thing: what happens to a photograph after you take it."

(Stand still. Nothing in your hands. That line buys you the room.)

2

The problem

0:15–0:50 · no screen

"You walk a building, you take eighty photographs. Then you get home and re-file them off the camera roll at nine at night, with a brew going cold. Then you write the findings up from memory. Then you type the reference in by hand and the page numbers by hand, and quote a regulation from memory because it's section four of something. Then you email it to six people — and now there are six versions. And the one in somebody's inbox is the version."

(Slow right down on "six versions". That's the line they nod at. Don't rush the next slide.)

The bitNobody in that story did anything wrong. It's just how the paper moves. Say that — it stops the room feeling accused.
3

What it is

0:50–1:15 · slide 3

"Same walk, paper moved to where the information already is. You capture on site. It drafts what the photograph shows. A person confirms every single one. It issues as a numbered document. It goes to the project's people. And every version stays exactly as it went out."

(Tap each of the six boxes as you say it. Slow and deliberate.)

The bitSix steps, and only one of them is the computer's. The other five are yours — that's the reassurance, so say it early.
4

Capture — it starts where you're standing

1:15–1:45 · your phone

"Step one. It starts where you're standing. This is the cockpit — opens off a QR code, installs to the home screen, because you're going to use it one-handed, in the rain, wearing gloves. Every photograph gets filed against the element, the room, the location as you take it. Those values stamp onto the photograph and become the location the report prints. Nothing to re-file. Nothing to email to yourself."

(Hold the phone up. Real beats slides every time — and if someone's close enough to see the screen, let them.)

The bitThe location goes on the photograph as you take it. That's the bit that saves the nine-at-night evening, and it's the one people repeat back to you afterwards.
5

The photograph becomes the writing

1:45–2:10 · SW-2026-10-001, one item

Open SW-2026-10-001 on the first tab, open one item, and point at the three things on screen: the finding text, the severity, the suggested trade.

"For each photograph it drafts a finding — what's there, what it means, what to do about it, and the likely cause when the picture actually supports one. It sets a severity and it suggests the trade. And everything it writes is marked as a draft, because that's what it is."

(Say "because that's what it is" flatly, not as a sales line. The honesty is what makes the next beat land.)

The bitIt's the same on a Monday morning as a Friday afternoon. It doesn't get tired, doesn't get rushed, and doesn't have an opinion about the contractor.
6

The gate — the one that matters

2:10–2:45 · the findings list

Show the confirm controls. If you've a draft open anywhere, show the app's own line: "Not ready to publish yet: N still not assessed, N with no responsible trade, N not confirmed by a person." Read it out loud.

"This is the part that matters. Nothing issues that a person hasn't confirmed. Every item is confirmed, corrected or deleted by somebody with a name. The report will not publish while anything is unassessed — that's a hard gate, not a warning banner. And where the photograph doesn't support a judgement, it says 'not assessed', and a person has to resolve it. It doesn't guess, and it doesn't fill the gap with confident language."

(This is the slide to slow down for. Two beats longer than feels natural. Never cut this one, whatever the clock says.)

The bitEvery confirmation carries a name and a time. If it ever comes to "who signed this off", the answer is on the record — not in somebody's memory.
7

The rule that matters most

2:45–3:20 · an item with a reference

Point at the regulation line on an item that has one.

"It may only cite what's on the list. Twelve Approved Documents — A, B, C, E, F, G, H, J, K, L, M, P. Six British and EN standards. Six HSE and statutory instruments. That's the ceiling. It picks the single most relevant one, from that list and nowhere else — and if nothing on the list fits, it cites nothing at all. No invented clauses. No guessed paragraph numbers."

(Pause before "no invented clauses" and let the room be quiet. This is the sentence the whole thing rests on.)

The bitIt would rather say nothing than guess a regulation. A blank line is honest; a wrong clause is a liability with your name on it. For anything going to a client — or a court — that's the whole ballgame.
8

What comes out

3:20–3:45 · the printed page / the PDF

Hold the printed page up. On screen: second tab → Open the report PDF.

"Cover page: project, client, address, reference, date, status, item count. Then item by item — numbered, graded, the action required, the photograph it was written from, and a pin marking the area it refers to."

(A paper report in the air beats any slide you will ever build.)

The bitThe pin. It marks the exact area on the photograph the item is about — so nobody stands on site arguing about which bit you meant.
The other bitSometimes it runs to forty-odd pages. That's deliberate: no item is ever split across a page break, and every photograph keeps its own room. More pages, nothing missed.
9

What was issued stays issued

3:45–4:05 · the version line on the cover

Point at "Published as version 1 on <date>".

"Publishing locks it. If you need to change it, you reopen it and it starts a new version — the issued one stays exactly as it went out, with its number and its date. So if somebody asks in three years what that report said, that's what it said. And references come off the database, so two reports can't claim the same one."

(Straight to camera. No slide-tapping.)

The bitIf they ask what the client holds: the version they were sent. Their link never gets quietly swapped for a newer one — a fresh link is a decision you make, not something that happens to them.
10

The register

4:05–4:25 · Projects → the register

Open Projects, the project, then its compliance register.

"Alongside the reports there's the weekly register. Week by week, the checks that were done — built from the same evidence as everything else, not reconstructed into a spreadsheet the week before an audit. It's the screen an auditor asks for, and it's already there when they ask."

(Pause on "already there".)

The bitMost people's audit prep is a fortnight of finding paperwork. Here the register was written as the weeks happened. That's the difference between an audit and a fire drill.
Keep it honestIt's a list of weeks and checks, not a chart that drew itself. Don't promise a graph.
11

The breadth

4:25–4:45 · the template chooser

Better than the slide: Start a report → the chooser. Five families, thirteen templates, each with one line saying what it's for.

"Thirteen templates in five families — condition, snagging and handover, site and safety, M&E, and records and registers. Thirty languages, including Arabic and Hebrew right-to-left, with English always kept as the record copy. Plus the trade and recipient directory, distribution, per-trade links, and your own branding on the document."

(It's a list — keep it moving. Don't let this slide become the speech.)

The bit worth having readyThirty languages means a Polish groundworker reads the same remedial list as the client, in Polish — and the English copy is still the one of record. Careful: the slide and the app now agree: thirteen templates in five families. The thirteenth is the health, safety & housekeeping inspection, added 14 October. Say thirteen.
12

The limits, and stop

4:45–5:00 · slide 12

"And what it doesn't do. It's not a drawing tool — no CAD, no BIM, no estimating. It doesn't decide engineering: severity, trade and sign-off are a person's. It won't invent a citation. And it won't send anything by itself — distribution is an action somebody takes. Everything it does is in one document. Thank you."

(Land "thank you" and stop. Don't add a sentence after it. The limits are why they trust the rest.)

If you get cut to three minutes

Drop 9 and 11. Cut 5 to one line: "the photograph becomes the writing, and everything it writes is a draft." Never drop 6 or 7 — the gate and the citation rule are the two things nobody else in the room can say.

If they ask

Short answers. Say one line and stop — a long answer sounds like a defence.

They askYou say
"Isn't this just ChatGPT writing my reports?""It drafts and a person confirms every item before anything can be published. Unassessed or unconfirmed, it won't issue. That's a gate, not a warning."
"How do you know it isn't making the regulation up?""It can only cite from a fixed list. Outside that list it cites nothing at all."
"What if the AI gets it wrong?""It can't publish by itself. The person confirming is the control — and the confirmation has their name on it."
"Can somebody change it after it's gone out?""No. What was issued stays issued. A change is a new version, and you decide whether they get it."
"Do we lose the paper trail?""You gain one. Every confirmation, every version, every send is recorded with who and when."
"How much is it?""I'll send you the commercial detail." Don't invent a figure.
"Does it do drawings or take-offs?""No, and it isn't trying to. It replaces the paperwork after the walk."

If it goes wrong in front of people

All recoverable. Say the honest line and carry on.

Share menu "isn't there" — you're on the first tab. It's on 2 Get PDF. "Let me get to the document view."

"Create link" is greyed out — that report isn't published yet, and the app won't make a client link to a draft. "It won't link a draft, by design." Switch to SW-2026-10-001.

The share link now hands over the finished document. Published 14 October: a link serves the frozen published version, so it can't drift when someone reopens the report. A recipient opening it gets the report page with Save your own copy - and that download is the frozen PDF. Practise it once before Friday.

Publish is refused — something's unassessed or unconfirmed. Read the line aloud; it names it. Then turn it into the point: "this is the gate."

Something's slow — "it's a live system, give it a moment", then talk to slide 3 while it catches up. Never tap anything twice in front of an audience.

Three things not to do

Don't publish live unless you mean it. It's a real record with your name on it, and a published version can't be unpublished.

Don't promise the sending works end to end. Emailing extracts hasn't been used on a live job yet. If asked: built, being proved on the first job. Costs you nothing and it's true.

Don't hold up a draft as finished — the register drafts, and DEMO-PC-001 (reopened, so not currently published) are the two places a sharp question could land.

The three things they should walk out remembering

1. "Nothing issues that a person hasn't confirmed."

2. "It may only cite what's on the list — and where nothing fits, it cites nothing at all."

3. "What was issued stays issued."
