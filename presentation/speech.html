The record of what you saw

Twelve slides, five minutes, spoken. Read the bold bits as written; the rest is yours.
SpeakerD. Hopwood
Runs5:00 (12 slides)
CompanionIB-PRES-001 deck
PaceSlow. Pause after each number.
1Title — the record of what you saw0:00–0:15

Morning. I'll keep this to five minutes, and I'll spend most of it on one thing: what happens to a photograph after you take it. Because that's where the paperwork goes wrong.

Do: stand still, no notes in hand. This line buys you the room.

2The problem — the paperwork happens twice0:15–0:50

Here's the way we all do it. You walk a building, you take eighty photographs. You get home and you re-file them from the camera roll at nine at night, with a cup of tea going cold. Then you write the findings up from memory. Then you type the reference in by hand, and the page numbers by hand. Then you quote a regulation from memory, because it's section four of something. And then you email it to six people — so now there are six versions, and the one in somebody's inbox is the version.

Nothing in that is anybody's fault. It's just the way the paper moves.

Do: slow down on "six versions". That's the line they'll nod at.

3What it is — the six-step flow0:50–1:15

This is the same walk, with the paper moved to where the information already is. You capture on site. It drafts what the photograph shows. A person confirms every single one. It issues as a numbered document. It goes to the project's people. And every version stays exactly as it went out.

Do: tap each of the six boxes as you say them. Slow, deliberate.

4Capture — it starts where you are standing1:15–1:45

Step one. It starts where you're standing. This is the cockpit — it opens from a QR code and it installs to the phone's home screen, because you're going to use it one-handed, in the rain, wearing gloves. Every photograph is filed against the element, the room, the location as you take it. Those values stamp onto the photograph and become the location the report prints. Nothing to re-file. Nothing to email to yourself.

Do: hold the phone up if you have it. Real beats slides.

5Draft — the photograph becomes the writing1:45–2:10

Step two. The photograph becomes the writing. For each one it drafts a finding — what's there, what it means, what to do about it, and the likely cause if the picture actually supports one. It sets a severity and it suggests the trade. And everything it writes is marked as a draft, because that's what it is.

Do: say "that's what it is" flatly. It's the honesty that makes the next slide land.

6The gate — nothing issues unconfirmed2:10–2:45

This is the part that matters. Nothing issues that a person has not confirmed. Every item is confirmed, corrected or deleted by somebody with a name. The report will not publish while anything is still unassessed — that's a hard gate, not a warning banner. And where the photograph doesn't support a judgement, it says not assessed, and a person has to resolve it. It doesn't guess, and it doesn't fill the gap with confident language.

Do: this is the slide to slow right down for. Two beats longer.

7The citation rule — only what is on the list2:45–3:20

That leads to the rule I'd want you to remember above all the others. It may only cite what is on the list. Twelve Approved Documents — A, B, C, E, F, G, H, J, K, L, M and P. Six British and EN standards. Six HSE and statutory instruments. That's the ceiling. It picks the single most relevant one, from that list and nowhere else — and if nothing on the list fits, it cites nothing at all. No invented clauses. No guessed paragraph numbers.

For a document that goes to a client, or a court, that's the whole ballgame.

Do: pause before "no invented clauses". Let it be quiet.

8The output — a document you can put in front of anyone3:20–3:45

And this is what comes out. Cover page: project, client, address, reference, date, status, item count. Then item by item — numbered, graded, with the action required, the photograph it was written from, and a pin marking the area it refers to. Twenty pages, twenty-three pages, whatever the job is.

Do: if you have a printed copy, hold it up. A paper report in the air beats any slide.

9Frozen — what was issued stays issued3:45–4:05

Publishing locks it. If you need to change it, you reopen it and it starts a new version — the issued one stays exactly as it went out, with its number and its date. So if somebody asks in three years what that report said, that's what it said. And references come off the database, so two reports can't claim the same one.

Do: to camera. No slide-tapping.

10Compliance — the register is built, not typed4:05–4:25

Alongside the reports there's the weekly compliance register. Week by week, the checks that were done — built from the same evidence as everything else, not reconstructed the week before an audit. It's the screen an auditor asks for, and it's already there when they ask.

Do: pause on "already there".

11Breadth — eleven, thirty, and the rest4:25–4:45

Eleven report types. Schedules of condition, property inventories, electrical, mechanical, damp, weatherproofing, snagging. Thirty languages, including Arabic and Hebrew right to left, with English always kept as the record copy. Plus the trade and recipient directory, distribution to the people who need it, and your own branding on the document.

Do: this is a list, so keep it moving. Don't let it become the speech.

12The limits, and the close4:45–5:00

And what it doesn't do. It's not a drawing tool — no CAD, no BIM, no estimating. It doesn't decide engineering — severity, trade and sign-off are a person's. It won't invent a citation. And it won't send anything by itself.

Everything it does is in one document — that's the code on the screen. Thank you.

Do: land "thank you" and stop. Don't add a sentence after it.

If you get cut to three minutes: drop slides 9 and 11, and cut slide 5 to one line ("the photograph becomes the writing; everything it writes is a draft"). Never drop slide 6 or slide 7 — the gate and the citation rule are the two things nobody else can say.
Notes on this script.
  • Every number is read from the product, not invented: 11 report types, 30 languages, 12 Approved Documents, 6 British/EN standards, 6 HSE or statutory instruments (source: the product's own definitions, 9 Oct 2026).
  • The reference figures for report length (20 and 23 pages) come from two real issued sample reports.
  • Slides 4, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 11 are built around screenshots you are taking. Until they are in, those slides show a labelled frame describing the shot.
  • Timing assumes roughly 145 words a minute with the pauses marked. Rehearse once with a timer; if you run long, the notes above say what to cut.
